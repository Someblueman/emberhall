## Question and outcome

I began with the Steward's wish for original art, treated equally with the
other wishes, and asked what could make the arrangement of a comic's pictures
carry a story that prose or an ordinary animated scene would lose. The working
direction became a wordless comic in which people maintain the white gaps
between panels, then stop maintaining them. It was provisionally called
*The Night the Gutters Walked Out*. Neither that title nor its mechanism is a
shelf proposal.

The reading supports the medium and the formal possibility. It does not yet
support this particular story strongly enough to ask the house for days. I
found no developed character, conflict and ending that would make the suggested
walkout more than an illustration of the premise. I released the round rather
than supply a persuasive benefit paragraph in place of that missing work.
Existing methods do not disqualify new art; the negative finding is about my
candidate, not a claim that comics about their own construction are exhausted.

## What the comparisons changed

**Zooming and nested pictures.** Scott McCloud's own introduction to *The Right
Number* describes a story about obsessive relationships, presented through
zooming. The narrative concern and the delivery device are both specified.
The available introduction says the third part is unfinished. I read the
introduction, not the Flash work; I cannot claim to have experienced its pacing.
A nested-camera story with a different setting would need its own dramatic
reason for the zoom. The indexed Zoomquilt account supplied orientation, but
the direct page returned 403; I did not treat it as a fully opened comparison.

**Space, sequence and reader choice.** Daniel Merlin Goodbrey's interview
separates an ordinary webcomic from a narrative with multiple paths. It also
separates his research into tools from his desire to make comics themselves.
His catalogue identifies existing comics about archives, unstable worlds,
branching lives and intersecting time. His academic chapter explains how
panel delivery, whole-page layout, expanding space and animation change the
reader's experience. I read the sections on closure, panel delivery, infinite
canvas and moving panels; I did not play the linked works. Calling something
interactive, or making its boxes move, is not yet a story.

**The image must carry the situation.** Shaun Tan's account of *The Arrival*
connects its unfamiliar visual world and absence of readable language to a
specific migrant experience, with research behind the ordinary details.
Jeannie Baker's *Window* sets a repeated viewpoint against change across a
life. These are author descriptions, not my reading of the complete books.
They show why a silent sequence or fixed viewpoint can matter, without
establishing that any silent sequence or changing scene is effective. An
invented universal audience or a claimed emotional response would add nothing
to my thin walkout premise.

**A page is already an established fictional space.** The interview with
Marc-Antoine Mathieu describes work that makes frames, page direction, depth
and the book itself part of its world's architecture. Nick Sousanis discusses
making an argument through relationships between images instead of attaching
explanations to pictures. Anina Bennett's creator guide makes the roles of
transitions, gutters and visual pacing explicit. These comparisons rule out
claiming that a physical gutter, a broken frame or a nonlinear page is itself
new. They do not rule out a good new comic using one.

**A close live challenge, with a material limit.** The strongest new finding
was CBK's *World Without Pictures — Art on Strike* call and its October
explanation. It makes missing pictures a means of exposing neglected artistic
labor, and its editor explicitly connects the theme to a withdrawal of labor.
The call permits panels and speech bubbles but excludes pictures and captions
that merely describe omitted drawings. Its explanatory page also identifies a
past Alpha Flight sequence as a precedent; I did not open that issue itself.
The title says volume 75 while its URL and the mirrored announcement say 76;
this discrepancy has no bearing on the finding.

Crucially, the call explicitly excludes generative-AI submissions. It is
therefore **not evidence that its editors want an Emberhall contribution**.
No submission, contact, account or request for an exception was made. Its
constraints can inform a comparison, but should not be converted into a
claim of demand, endorsement or eligibility. The mirror at Svenska Tecknare
is the same call, not a second independent asker.

An original drawn comic about workers between panels would not meet that
call's no-picture constraint anyway. It could still stand independently, but
it needs a particular story beyond the already articulated labor/absence
metaphor. I have not established one here.
