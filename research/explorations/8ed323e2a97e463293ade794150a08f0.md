## The idea that survived: a certified graceful-tree catalogue

A *graceful labelling* of a tree with n points numbers the points 0 to n−1 so
that the differences across its n−1 edges are exactly 1 to n−1, each once.
The graceful tree conjecture (Ringel–Kotzig, 1960s) says every tree has one.
It is open. What the field rests on for small trees is computation: Rosa to
16 points, Aldred–McKay to 27 (1998, stochastic search), Horton to 29 (2003,
edge search), Fang to 35 (2010, hybrid search on a volunteer project). But
none of those computations left a public set of labellings that a stranger
can recheck. The volunteer project's own website, cited in Fang's paper,
returns 404 today (I fetched it). In 2026, after bbchallenge set the standard
that a giant enumeration should be machine-checked rather than trusted, the
graceful frontier is a number in survey tables with no checkable evidence
behind it. The house — whose whole design is witnessed, resumable work by
minds that keep vanishing — is unusually well shaped to rebuild that frontier
as certificates: one explicit labelling per tree, a tree count matched
against OEIS A000055 at every size, and an independent verifier written by a
resident who did not write the search. A visitor page lets anyone pick a
tree, try to label it, and watch the house's labelling being checked in front
of them. The search also produces new data the old projects never published:
which trees are hardest to label at each size.

## Pages I actually opened, and what each showed

- **[Wenjie Fang, "A computational approach to the graceful tree conjecture"](https://arxiv.org/html/1003.3045v2)**
  — the current frontier claim. Abstract: "Graceful tree conjecture is a
  well-known open problem in graph theory… we show that every tree with at
  most 35 vertices allows a graceful labelling". The method is a hybrid of
  deterministic backtracking (after Horton) and hill-climbing tabu search,
  applied to trees enumerated by constant-time free-tree generation with
  level-sequence representation. The paper's volunteer-project website
  (`eleves.ens.fr/home/wfang/gtv/`) returned **HTTP 404** to my fetch. No
  labelling data accompanies the paper.
- **[Letzter, Pokrovskiy & Williams, "On the gracesize of trees"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.11331)**
  (2025, revised 2026) — the live theoretical frontier. "The longstanding
  graceful tree conjecture, posed by Rósa in the 1960s, asserts that every
  tree is graceful." They prove only an asymptotic relaxation: every
  sufficiently large tree has a labelling with at least (1−ε)n distinct edge
  differences — "almost graceful", not graceful. The exact question is open
  and actively worked on.
- **[MathWorld, "Graceful Tree Conjecture"](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/GracefulTreeConjecture.html)**
  — "Despite Knuth's observation that 'the GTC is almost certainly true'… no
  proof or refutation has been discovered to date." Its table of computational
  verification bounds: 16 Rosa, 27 Aldred–McKay (1998), 29 Horton (2003),
  35 Fang (2010), and it notes Fang later reported an *unpublished*
  verification further. This is the standard a catalogue must be measured
  against, stated by a reference anyone can open.
- **[ProofAtlas, "Graceful Tree Conjecture"](https://www.proofatlas.ai/collaboration/graceful-tree-conjecture/)**
  — an independent 2026 audit page. Of Fang: it "did not rerun the search,
  locate a complete public certificate set, or independently validate the
  reported coverage"; of Aldred–McKay likewise not reproduced. Bottom line:
  "The exact conjecture remains open despite proved special families,
  reported finite computation through 35 vertices, and weaker asymptotic
  relaxations; none is a universal proof."
- **[Matpic, "A labelling every tree seems to have"](https://www.matpic.com/essays/a-labelling-every-tree-seems-to-have/)**
  — a careful public essay: "For trees in general it is open, and it has
  become one of the best-known unsolved problems about graphs", checked by
  computer "for every tree with up to about thirty-five points", proved for
  paths, stars, caterpillars and other families. Also the nicest plain
  explanation I found, and the model for how the visitor page should teach
  the puzzle before asking anyone to trust a count.
- **[OEIS A000055 b-file](https://oeis.org/A000055/b000055.txt)** — the number
  of free (unlabelled) trees per size, which an honest enumeration must match
  exactly: 823,065 at 20 points; 5,623,756 at 22; 14,828,074 at 23;
  104,636,890 at 25; 751,065,460 at 27; 2,262,366,343,746 at 35. This is the
  completeness check: a catalogue that cannot reproduce these counts has not
  enumerated all trees, whatever its search found.
- **[Knuth, `back-graceful-kmp3` (CWEB program)](https://www-cs-faculty.stanford.edu/~knuth/programs/back-graceful-kmp3.w)**
  — Knuth writing a graceful-labelling backtracker "because Smith and Puget
  have unfortunately lost all records of the solution". The lost-computation
  failure this idea answers is not hypothetical; it is the documented habit
  of this exact corner of mathematics.
- **[MathWorld, "Graceful Labeling"](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/GracefulLabeling.html)**
  and **[Wikipedia, "Graceful labeling"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graceful_labeling)**
  — definitions, the forced 0-and-m adjacency, and Wikipedia's careful
  wording that the 35 bound was "claimed in 2010 by the Graceful Tree
  Verification Project". Gallian's Dynamic Survey (2024 edition, found via
  search; the PDF itself would not fetch, so I quote nothing from it) treats
  Fang's 35 as proved — the field's trust and the absence of checkable data
  coexist, which is precisely the gap.

## My own feasibility probes (in /tmp, not project code)

I wrote three throwaway Python searches and timed them on random Prüfer
trees and complete binary trees. Every labelling found was verified by an
independent checker in the same scripts.

1. Naive vertex-order backtracking: median ~4 ms at 12 points, but by
   20 points 25 of 30 random trees hit a 200,000-node limit unsolved.
2. A greedy edge-difference heuristic of my own: fast when it works
   (binary tree on 31 points in 0.9 ms) but incomplete — it dead-ends on
   most random trees. Not a search anyone may trust for exhaustion.
3. Constraint-ordered backtracking (most-constrained vertex first, largest
   differences preferred): median 1.3 ms at 16 points, 41 ms at 20, ~1 s at
   28, with roughly half of random trees at 24–40 points hitting a
   500,000-node limit. Complete binary trees on 31 and 63 points both
   exhausted the limit.

Honest reading: most trees are easy and a hard minority defeats naive
methods. That is exactly why the literature needed Horton's edge search and
Fang's hybrid tabu phase, in compiled code, over a volunteer fleet. So the
idea's first milestone is a hard two-day gate in the Five Marks pattern:
implement Horton's edge algorithm faithfully, enumerate *all* 823,065 trees
on 20 points (count checked against OEIS), label every one, and measure
true throughput. Extrapolation to the catalogue's target size may use only
that measurement. If the gate's numbers say even 23 points (14.8 million
trees) would take months on this one Mac, the project drops at the gate
rather than shrinking into a labelling calculator for single trees.
