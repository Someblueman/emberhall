## Direction 1: warnings under a changing instrument

Proposed question: can a warning distinguish an approaching transition from a
change in how the same underlying process is measured? The possible deliverable
was a reproducible simulation experiment with fixed alarm rules, matched
non-tipping controls, and future-horizon scores rather than retrospective plots.

The need is real. The 2024 review discusses hindsight, inconsistent significance
tests and instrument changes. But Smith et al. already study instrument noise,
synthetic processes, real satellite records and aggregation order, with archived
code. A generic simulator or comparison table would repeat substantial work.
Du Plessis et al. also explicitly frame finite-horizon scoring and suitable
ensembles. I did not establish a precise remaining experiment that warrants
weeks. A newer irreversibility method might change this assessment, but I could
not inspect its full corrected methods: that is an unresolved reading boundary,
not grounds for claiming novelty or rejecting its results.

Opened and read:

- [Smith et al., 2023](https://esd.copernicus.org/articles/14/173/2023/):
  methods 2.1, results and conclusions; direct predecessor for the proposed work.
- [Dakos et al., 2024](https://esd.copernicus.org/articles/15/1117/2024/esd-15-1117-2024.html):
  discussion 4.1 and 4.2.1; identifies methodological and measurement limitations.
- [Du Plessis et al., 2026 preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.01677v1):
  sections 3 and 4.5; testable forecast horizons and ensemble-design challenges.
- [Dalle Nogare and Fulcher, 2026](https://journals.aps.org/prresearch/abstract/10.1103/4nb2-398s):
  abstract; broad comparison of irreversibility statistics is already published.

## Direction 2: can ecosystem models warn when their forecasts will fail?

Proposed question: does disagreement among equally well-fitting ecosystem
models identify unreliable forecasts on previously unseen microcosms? The
possible deliverable was a preregistered, composition-grouped evaluation of
forecast error, uncertainty and abstention against persistence and simple models.

Botelho et al. document a serious calibration-versus-prediction problem. Their
110 microcosms have daily abundance observations over 19 days. Crucially, their
press interventions are simulated: agreement among models is measured without
observed intervention outcomes. Those data cannot establish that a selected
conservation action works. A narrower abundance-forecast study remains possible,
but Wesselkamp et al. already supply a forecast-limit framework, reference-model
comparisons and explicit discussion of spread versus error. I found no specific
new result or clearly justified experimental contrast sufficient to defend this
as a weeks-long project. Failure to find one is provisional, not a literature-wide
priority claim. The authors' clarification also rejects the sweeping conclusion
that all ecosystem models are unreliable; this screening preserves that limit.

Opened and read:

- [Botelho et al., preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.10439):
  methods on observed data, held-out abundance and simulated interventions;
  discussion limits. Do not treat simulated intervention agreement as accuracy.
- [Authors' 2025 clarification](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ele.70175):
  scope of their negative result and call for independent validation.
- [Wesselkamp et al., 2025](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/2041-210X.70049):
  case-study table, spread-error discussion and future comparison requirements.
- [Pennekamp data repository](https://github.com/pennekampster/Code_and_data_OverallEcosystemStability):
  README and data-directory listing; observational source exists, with CC0 data
  and separately licensed analysis code. No files were downloaded or executed.
