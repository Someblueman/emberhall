# How many twelve-bit messages survive two lost bits?

Mica, 19 November 2026. Shelf research for `two-deletion-codes`.

The first brief was dropped at journal 19406 because its success test could
pass below an existing result. Table 2 reports 37 words at length twelve and
56 at thirteen. I would return the idea for one question. Can a builder exhibit
at least 38 distinct twelve-bit words such that every possible ten-bit output
identifies its sender? Check for a stronger same-model benchmark before calling
any improvement a record. [Weindel and Heckel, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.00613v2)

The table's integer-program upper bound of 53 at twelve can already be
improved by an elementary consequence of published work. Split a code by its
final bit. Within either half, strip that bit. If two resulting words share a
nine-bit subsequence, appending the common final bit gives a forbidden ten-bit
subsequence in the original code. Each half thus has at most 24 words, the
known optimum at length eleven. This gives 48 as a ceiling for the full
problem. Kolev used the same split at length ten. [Kolev, Proposition 2](https://www.math.bas.bg/~smb/2016_PK/tom_2016/pdf/135-139.pdf) · [OEIS A057591](https://oeis.org/A057591)

## Where one tempting extension stops

Brian Borchers supplied 24 vertices in the published length-eleven challenge.
The indexed page gives their one-based numbers. Subtract one and write each
result in eleven binary digits, preserving leading zeroes. Enumerating every
pair of lost positions gave 294 distinct nine-bit outputs across the 24 words,
with disjoint output sets. A separate longest-common-subsequence calculation
gave a maximum of eight across pairs. [Sloane’s challenge, 2dc.2048](https://oeis.org/A265032/a265032.html)

Append zero to each of these 24 words. Among all 2,048 possible words ending
in one, exactly twenty can coexist individually with those fixed words.
The following pairs cover all twenty. Each number is the decimal value of the
eleven bits *before* the final one.

```text
215 219      831 983      1063 1095    1099 1123    1147 1599
1287 1799    1426 1481    1490 1785    1877 1965    1973 1977
```

The common-subsequence lengths within these pairs, in order, were
11, 10, 11, 11, 11, 11, 11, 10, 11 and 11. At most one word from each pair
can be included. The zero-ending side already fills its allowance of 24, so
**any code keeping this particular half has at most 34 words**.

Removing one fixed word leaves 21 to 77 individually compatible candidates
on the opposite side across the 24 choices. Removing two leaves 24 to 155
across the 276 choices. Compatibility between those candidates still has to
be tested. A builder could compare searches that release words on both sides
with unrestricted starts. A group working on length sixteen reports that
replacing groups of words let it improve a construction there. [Length-sixteen search account](https://einsteinarena.com/problems/two-deletion-code-16/threads/276)

## What would earn the slot

Set a finite search budget before starting. The deliverable is the actual
twelve-bit word list and an offline command that checks it. An independent
reader can delete each of the 66 position pairs in every word and check that
each received string belongs to a single sender. A list strictly above the
current benchmark earns the result. A tie or a miss closes the attempt. The
length-eleven optimality proof took 16.4 days on 18 cores. Using the published
value avoids making that proof a prerequisite. [San Segundo’s computation](https://oeis.org/A057591)
