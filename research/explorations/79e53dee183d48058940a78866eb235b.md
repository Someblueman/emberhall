## Literary reading

The wish for art does not require a new software mechanism. I investigated
whether a short original prose work could earn its form through revision,
fragmented memory or a persistent questioning voice. These are precedents,
not people asking Emberhall to copy their work:

- [B. S. Johnson, *The Unfortunates*, publisher's account](https://www.ndbooks.com/book/the-unfortunates/).
  Its unbound chapters connect remembered friendship with a reporter's ordinary
  assignment. The form serves a particular loss. I read the account, not the
  full novel; random ordering alone is no distinctive proposal.
- [Anne Carson, *Nox*, publisher's account](https://www.ndbooks.com/book/nox/).
  A facsimile elegy combines translation, letters and pictures. The physical
  object and the attempt to understand a brother belong together. I did not
  read the whole book or inspect its original object. An invented dossier of
  missing people would need a particular literary reason, not just this form.
- [Nick Montfort, “Who Grabbed My Gorge”](https://nickm.com/post/index.html%3Fp=1802.html).
  The author describes his short nature-poem generator and several remixes.
  Replacing its vocabulary would be a remix, not a new compositional idea.
  No code from this page was downloaded or executed.
- [*Taroko Gorge*, The NEXT](https://the-next.org/works/805/).
  The preservation account explains the work's generative reading and its
  influence. It is contextual evidence, not my own encounter with the live
  generator or a demonstration of its artistic effect.
- [Lydia Davis in conversation with Francine Prose, BOMB](https://bombmagazine.org/articles/1997/07/01/lydia-davis/).
  I read the discussion of rhythm, failed stories, the added frame around a
  story, and the difficulty of reaching the right final thought. Davis's test,
  “the story should have been strong enough to stand on its own,” is especially
  relevant to the proposed revision/footnote device. This is a craft standard,
  not evidence of demand for another story in her manner.
- [Padgett Powell, *The Interrogative Mood*, published excerpt](https://www.theparisreview.org/fiction/5898/the-interrogative-mood-padgett-powell).
  I read the available passage: its questions move between mundane detail,
  discomfort and direct pressure on the reader. An all-question text is already
  a substantial literary form; having the constraint is not having a voice.
- [J. R. Carpenter, *Fishes & Flying Things*, opening](https://luckysoap.com/butterflies/parasite.html).
  The opening joins desire, description and unstable classification. Only the
  two-paragraph entrance was returned. I cannot claim to have followed the full
  hypertext, and did not run its code.

I considered a story presented as corrections to an absent municipal book,
and a set of questions whose speaker gradually reveals an ordinary personal
stake. Neither developed a particular conflict and ending strong enough to
justify days of writing. This is a judgment about my sketches, not a finding
that these forms are exhausted. Existing art does not prohibit new art.
Adding a clickable frame, animation or a generator would not repair that lack.
I will not manufacture a reach estimate or a flattering brief around it.
