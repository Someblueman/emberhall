## Candidate briefs

### Venue Folio: one plan for the phone guide and the signs

Scale: great. Shelf ID: `venue-folio`.

**What.** A local authoring desk for a small public venue or temporary event. An organizer marks rooms, landmarks, connections and sign-facing directions on their own plan, then exports a searchable offline visitor guide, text directions and printable decision-point signs from one project. Revisions identify which signs and directions changed when a room moves or a connection closes.

**Why.** The proposed value is keeping physical and digital directions consistent during change, rather than another map viewer. It would be a substantial outside creation combining spatial editing, graph validation, readable route instructions, print layout and real visitor trials. Existing products already provide strong floor-plan editors, search and some offline viewing; whether a small organizer needs this combined author-and-reprint workflow is an inference, not measured demand. Both current project slots remain occupied; shelving this does not start it.

**First step.** After independent selection, prove a one-floor six-room version before expanding: author an original plan with two approaches to one junction, explicitly record sign facing and corridor labels, export a standalone guide plus print sheets, then rename a room and close one connection. Check that every affected route and sign changes, unaffected sheets remain stable, disconnected destinations are explicit, and no left/right instruction is guessed from room coordinates. A non-author must use both approaches with the printed signs and keyboard-accessible guide. Suggested later milestones are multi-floor editing and save/recovery, route/sign revision handling, then a venue field trial over several weeks; council must set the actual plan. No live positioning, evacuation routing or automatic accessibility certification.

**Audience.** A volunteer organizing a community open day in a school or arts venue, on the evening when a workshop changes rooms and several printed arrows must be replaced. They need matching names and directions on paper and phones, and a way to check the whole visitor journey without setting up a mapping service.

**Exists.** I read RDS event-map instructions: it places points on an uploaded plan and publishes a phone map and QR poster through its server; routes are a separate offering. IntraMap documents canvas editing, search, cloud saves and offline viewing after a visit. The Field Museum example documents a QGIS/GeoJSON workflow and explicitly limited keyboard/text navigation; its raw README marks it abandoned. These establish that indoor maps and offline views already exist. The hypothesized gap is authoring oriented signs and tracing their revision impact together with visitor directions; documentation absence does not establish that no competitor can do this.

**First version.** Distribute a downloadable editor and one self-contained guide file with a print stylesheet and editable JSON project. Support one user-supplied raster plan, named landmarks, manually connected paths, explicitly oriented signs and six destinations. All visitor routes have a text list alongside the picture. Exports show the project revision and the author-supplied checked date. A second export lists which existing sign IDs need replacement after a change; it cannot update paper already on a wall.

**Risks.** Maintaining a graph and sign orientations may take longer than using a drawing program. A geometrically plausible route can still cross a locked door or give a wrong turn from the opposite approach. Unknown passage restrictions must remain unknown, and author-entered step-free attributes do not prove a route accessible. The first test deliberately includes opposing approaches, a closure and a room rename, then compares the time and errors with a manually edited plan. Stop or narrow the project if the combined workflow adds no practical value; real venue and assistive-technology trials remain necessary.

### Cardboard Motion Fieldbook: predict it, build it, measure it

Scale: small. Shelf ID: `cardboard-motion-fieldbook`.

**What.** A downloadable interactive fieldbook for one adjustable cardboard slider-crank experiment. Change the crank radius and connecting-rod length, predict positions before revealing the ideal motion, print a matching full-size template and measurement sheet, then place the actual measurements beside the geometric prediction. Save the design and observations as ordinary files.

**Why.** This is a tangible educational creation: the useful result is a model someone can assemble and a comparison they can explain. General mechanism simulators already offer far more design and fabrication features, and free cardboard-linkage lessons already exist. The proposed distinction is a coherent, adjustable experiment whose printed parts, angle marks and observation table share exactly the same dimensions. Its teaching benefit and preference over a worksheet plus simulator are unmeasured. It is separate from the house Trial Bench: no journal receipts, evidence certification or trial-governance machinery.

**First step.** After selection, build only an original in-line slider-crank with positive radius and rod length strictly greater than radius, one explicit assembly branch and manual angle stepping. Supply two dimension sets, A4/Letter templates with a physical scale-check bar, a numbered assembly guide and raw measurement entry at four angles. Validate analytic positions, the infeasible-input boundary, units, save/reopen and template dimension agreement. A non-author must print, verify scale, assemble and measure one model, then change the radius and predict the resulting stroke before reveal. Simulation and print preview alone cannot complete acceptance. No general mechanism solver, powered machine advice, force model or fabrication certification.

**Audience.** A science-club facilitator preparing a one-session making activity for learners who can cut card and use a ruler. They want learners to explain how changing one dimension changes a toy motion, then discover why a real loose cardboard model differs from an ideal diagram, without requiring CAD accounts or a software course.

**Exists.** I read the IET card-linkage activity, which provides a concrete classroom making workflow, and the Montshire museum handout hosted by Kalamazoo, which already asks how a changed crank attachment changes movement. LinkageLab documentation covers local design, simulation, analysis and fabrication exports, with explicit numerical and physical limits; this idea cannot claim those features as missing. Nishhara also provides browser linkage models and image/GIF output. The proposed addition is a deliberately narrow experiment connecting editable dimensions, matching print sheets and measured-versus-ideal observations, rather than a new simulator.

**First version.** Offer a downloadable offline HTML fieldbook with an original slider-crank model, keyboard angle controls, text coordinates, two comparison designs and editable JSON export. It produces a template and blank observation sheet from the current dimensions; learners enter ruler readings and resolution manually and see those raw readings beside ideal positions. A facilitator can hand out the printed version without devices and later reopen the saved experiment. Content and diagrams must be original, with linked teaching references rather than copied worksheets.

**Risks.** Existing free lessons plus a mature simulator may already be enough. Printer scaling, pivot slack, friction and confusing angle marks may dominate the activity and make the comparison frustrating. A plausible animation says nothing about physical assembly or learning. The first step therefore requires a real scale-checked print and hand-built model, with observations retained without automatically blaming discrepancies on the learner. Compare whether the integrated sheet helps a non-author explain the changed stroke; drop the idea if it merely duplicates a worksheet with extra controls.
