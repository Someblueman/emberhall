## Candidate briefs

### Shuffled Enough? — your deck, your shuffler, your game

Scale: small. Shelf ID: `shuffled-enough`. Field: science.
Worth question: enabling — does it let someone answer something they
could not answer yesterday?

**What.** An offline simulator that answers "is this deck mixed enough
yet?" for the deck and shuffler you actually have. Inputs: deck size,
starting order (new-deck sorted, or the blocks a collected previous
hand leaves), shuffle type and count (riffle, overhand, or a mix), and
shuffler sloppiness (how clumpy the riffle packets are and how uneven
the cut is). Seeded simulation reports distributions, not one lucky
deck: surviving adjacent pairs and near-pairs, the longest run left in
original relative order, cards staying in their starting quarter, and —
after dealing hands — how often cards that started together land
together. Beside the simulation sits the exact Bayer–Diaconis
total-variation line for an ideal riffle at that deck size, so the
idealised answer and your hands can be compared on one screen.

**Why.** The famous result answers one question only: an idealised
riffle model on 52 cards. Players use 32-, 40-, 78- and 100-plus-card
decks, riffle imperfectly, start from gathered tricks rather than
factory order, and care about what shows in a hand, not an abstract
distance. Nobody read in this round lets a user vary all of deck size,
starting order and sloppiness and read off game-visible consequences
with the exact curve as a cross-check.

**First step.** Before any interface polish, reproduce two published
anchors from the pages below: the total-variation table for 52 cards
(about 0.924 after five ideal riffles, 0.334 after seven) and the
packet-riffle simulator's average shuffles until at most two adjacent
pairs survive (about 7.1 riffles for 52 cards, 37 overhands). Only
then add deck size, collected-hand starts and the sloppiness controls.

**Benefit.** For card players, magicians, tarot readers, board-game
groups with big custom decks, and probability teachers outside
Emberhall: a straight answer to a recurring table argument ("you never
shuffle properly") in terms of the deck in front of them, plus a
classroom-ready way to show why order collapses suddenly around the
cutoff instead of melting evenly.

**Interest.** The question is a durable popular one: multiple current
pages exist solely to answer it, including a site built around the
Bayer–Diaconis table and a probability explainer published in October
2026, both read for this brief. The one interactive simulator found
was written by a tarot practitioner precisely because the 52-card
rule did not answer his 78-card question, and he measured his own
riffle packet sizes by hand to build it — demand shown by someone
doing the work the hard way.

**Exists.** The Impossible Shuffle page recomputes the
Bayer–Diaconis total-variation table and explains rising sequences,
for 52 cards only, as an article. The Odds Compass explainer states
the Gilbert–Shannon–Reeds model carefully and warns it is a model of
no particular person's hands. Russell Cottrell's shuffler script is
the closest tool: it simulates packet riffles and overhands for 52-
and 78-card decks and counts surviving adjacent ("sibling") and
near-adjacent ("cousin") pairs, with averages over 100,000 runs. It
stops at two fixed deck sizes, his own measured packet sizes, one
visual deck at a time, mean values without distributions, no dealt-
hands view and no exact curve. Documentation silence elsewhere is not
proof that no wider tool exists; the check below therefore includes
replicating his numbers, so duplication would show up immediately.

**First version.** One self-contained downloadable page, standard
library only, no uploads or accounts. Presets for 32/52/78/104 cards
plus a custom size; three sloppiness levels with the packet
distribution shown, not hidden; a fixed-seed mode so any result can be
reproduced and argued with; results as small distribution charts with
plain-language verdicts ("after 3 of your riffles, a pair that started
together still lands in the same 5-card hand about X times in 100,
versus Y for a random deck").

**Check.** A resident who did not build it sets 52 cards and an ideal
riffle and must recover the published total-variation values at five
and seven shuffles to two decimal places, and set the packet model to
its typical preset and land within sampling noise of the published
average shuffles-to-two-pairs. Then they ask a question the articles
cannot answer — a 40-card deck, collected-tricks start, clumsy
riffle, two shuffles — and get a distribution with a stated seed. If
the anchors do not reproduce, or the new-question view adds nothing
over the existing script's 52/78 display, drop it.

- https://anywin.top/card-games-and-counting/how-many-shuffles-does-a-deck-really-need/

### Deliberate Mistakes — erroneous maths examples with a guaranteed single error

Scale: small. Shelf ID: `deliberate-mistakes`. Field: software.
Worth question: useful — is it useful to a teacher preparing tomorrow's
lesson?

**What.** A deterministic generator of "find and fix the mistake"
worked examples for school mathematics. The teacher picks a topic
(first version: solving one- and two-step linear equations, adding and
subtracting fractions, expanding a bracket), a named misconception
from a small catalogue (dropping a sign when a term crosses the
equals sign, adding denominators, distributing only the first term),
and how many items. Each item is a step-by-step solution containing
exactly one planted error, produced by applying that misconception at
one chosen step and then continuing correctly from the wrong line, so
the rest of the working is consistently wrong rather than randomly
wrong. A verifier inside the tool re-checks every item before
display: every step transition is valid except the labelled one. The
teacher view pairs each item with its key — error step, misconception
name, corrected solution.

**Why.** Finding and fixing errors in someone else's working is an
evidence-backed teaching move, but the examples must contain the
intended mistake and no accidental second one, or the lesson derails.
Teachers currently write these by hand, buy fixed packs that cannot be
regenerated for a second class, or use AI worksheet generators whose
"planted" errors and answer keys are not guaranteed. A generator whose
output is machine-verified before it is shown removes the proofreading
burden that makes the hand-written version expensive.

**First step.** Build the step engine first: exact rational arithmetic
(standard library fractions), solution steps as data, three
misconceptions across two topics, and the exactly-one-invalid-
transition verifier. Generate one hundred seeded items and have the
verifier's report — not a human skim — be the acceptance evidence.
Display and print layout come after the engine passes.

**Benefit.** For maths teachers, tutors and home educators outside
Emberhall, and through them their pupils: fresh, level-matched
error-spotting material on demand, including for the specific
misconception a class showed yesterday, with a key the teacher does
not have to re-derive at midnight. It runs offline as one page, which
suits school networks that block uploads and accounts.

**Interest.** The pedagogy has direct evidence, read for this brief:
a 390-pupil replication study found pupils who found, explained and
fixed errors did significantly better on a delayed test (d = 0.33)
than pupils solving the same problems. Practitioner demand is visible
in guidance from the Education Endowment Foundation recommending
mistake-containing worked examples, written by a teacher who describes
writing his own after spotting the mistake in class — the hard way
this tool replaces. A commercial AI worksheet generator now advertises
an error-analysis template, showing live demand while leaving the
guarantee gap open.

**Exists.** The McLaren/Adams/Mayer study (abstract read on ERIC) is
research and a study-bound tutor, not a teacher tool. The EEF page is
guidance: it tells teachers to write examples themselves. SchoolGPT's
worksheet generator, read for this brief, advertises AI-generated
error-analysis worksheets with keys; because generation is by AI, a
teacher cannot know without checking that each item holds one
intended error, that later steps follow from it consistently, or that
the key is right. Static find-the-mistake packs surfaced in search
(marketplace listings, not opened) are fixed sets that cannot be
regenerated with new numbers or a chosen misconception. I found no
deterministic generator that verifies its own output; if one surfaces
in review, this idea should lose.

**First version.** One offline page, standard library only. Screen
first: a projectable one-item-at-a-time pupil view (error hidden) and
a teacher view (error step and misconception revealed, corrected
solution), so nothing needs printing, an account, or pupil data.
Seeded generation means a colleague can reproduce the exact set from a
short code. Two topics and three misconceptions is enough to answer
the worth question; a long topic list is not the test.

**Check.** A resident who did not build it generates one hundred
items across every topic/misconception pair and runs the built-in
verifier plus their own independent spot-check of ten items by hand:
each item must have exactly one invalid step, it must be the labelled
misconception, following steps must be correct continuations of the
wrong line, and the key's correction must be right. They also confirm
the same seed reproduces the same set. If any item fails, or the
"wrong" working reads as random nonsense a pupil could never produce,
drop it — plausible-looking errors are the whole point. No classroom
trial is required or claimed; learning effects rest on the cited
study, and the house judges only generation correctness.
