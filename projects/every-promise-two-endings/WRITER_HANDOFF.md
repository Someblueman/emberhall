# Writer's handoff

Spoilers follow. This is a continuity note, not independent literary acceptance.

The chapter is an evening negotiation followed by the next day's crossing and
meal. Iris interprets between people who already share a language. Her brother
Ivo disappeared on the night ferry seven weeks ago; his coat was recovered.
His fate is deliberately unresolved. Do not turn a later revelation into a
reward or punishment for choosing a promise.

Edda, an upland baker, has been feeding the evacuated people of Lowbank. She
wants her sister Runa to be a neighbour again instead of a grateful recipient.
Runa wants to pass something back; she also promises to bring her neighbour
Pet to the meal. Pet's son is missing. Pet can understand an agreement and
still be hurt by what it asks of her.

Dan built the bridge and needs to finish something, but he has a real engineering
concern. His daughter Mara is angry that he stayed at the mill during the flood
while she carried his wife uphill. The wife survived; this did not erase the
argument. Mara's help is an action she chooses, not automatic forgiveness.
Sava keeps the evacuation register. Her husband disappeared in an earlier
flood. She remembers losing her temper when a child took his empty chair.

The first promise chooses a watched crossing at dawn or inspection and bracing
in daylight. The first gives Runa and Pet an early crossing and costs Dan an
emergency stop. The second costs time, bread and patience, and lets Dan show Mara
his work. In both, Mara acts; neither produces a casualty or a tidy reconciliation.

The second promise keeps empty places or reads names and frees the chairs.
An unknowing child tests both versions. In the first, Edda tries to protect Iris's
promise, and Iris shares her chair. In the second, Pet hears haste in words she
fully understood; Runa has to stop explaining and Sava sits with her. The four
combined readings have separately conditioned after-scenes. The ending returns
to the smaller promise of bringing a lamp back.

## Middle chapters (Hawthorn)

**What we carry upstairs** begins three days later. Pet asks for relief blankets
stored above the mill's waterline. The first new promise gives Mara the lead
after a joint inspection, or asks Dan and Mara to enter together and require
both voices before moving a load. The second keeps the blankets at the school
while freeing a classroom, or opens Edda's upstairs rooms to willing movers.
Neither option transfers people without asking them. Pet stays at the school
on the bakery branch; Runa cannot use her relocation as proof of success.

Earlier words cause later actions. The dawn crossing makes Mara put the bale
down at a noise; the daylight inspection gives her a chalk-mark method and a
way to work with Dan. The chair promise changes Iris's offer of a stool to Pet;
the pages promise makes Pet stop another pre-emptive explanation, then use a
new list on her own terms. The optional interviews deepen those exchanges but
are not required for the plot to make sense. The boy now has a name, Tavi;
Pet's son is Neri. Do not conflate them or use Tavi as a replacement son.

**The sound a thing keeps** follows the next day. The household store room is
inspected and reopened. Iris's father's violin survives with an open seam and
fallen bridge; it cannot simply be tuned and played. Its recovery says nothing
about Ivo's fate. Iris puts her request behind the shared work, then admits she
might play again badly, for herself. No magical repair or missing-person reveal.

One revisable promise offers a public afternoon of recognising belongings or
private household requests at the mill door. The public table returns more
things and exposes a photograph its living owner did not choose to show; Anet
limits further exposure, without erasing what was seen. Private requests keep
parcels out of sight, but take longer and miss things people cannot describe.
Both leave unclaimed belongings stored; nobody wins the property of the absent.

Yel, Dan's wife, is now present as a person. She says she heard the argument
during the flood when she could not get her legs to move. This gives the family
another experience to hear; it does not explain Dan's choice away or settle the
argument. Mara's private afternoon at the end is deliberately not disclosed to
Iris. The characters should acquire more of this life beyond shared work.

## Chapter four: Where the key hangs (Gorse)

**Where the key hangs** begins two mornings after the store-room return. The mill
key hangs on the hook inside the bakery door with a square of brown paper behind
it. Runa and Edda are now living inside the shelter arrangement chosen in chapter
two:
- On the school branch, Runa crosses at six with the school's bread list before
  Lise begins lessons in the west room; Edda leaves the flour ledger open so
  Runa checks weights herself, and their disagreements are ordinary shopkeeper's
  work. At the bridge gate Runa admits she sleeps better at the school where no
  one listens for her feet; Edda admits the house is too quiet at nine o'clock
  and tells her not to come over out of pity, only on Thursday with a sharp pencil.
- On the bakery branch, Runa lights the oven at four, has burnt the back of her
  thumb on the latch, and sweeps around the four pairs of boots on the landing
  without moving them. When Marten upstairs tries to leave a copper coin for the
  coal, Runa returns it and tells him to complain about the crust like anyone
  else. Edda lets Runa take the heavier end of the jar crate.

By the tailrace, Yel and Mara speak without Iris administering them. Iris stops
by the seasoning timber and listens without interrupting. Mara says her private
afternoon was up at High Fold, cutting loom pegs from larch windfall with Else.
Yel does not ask her to stay at the mill; instead Yel tells Mara that when the
water came under their door she was in the cellar salting late cabbages and had
brine to her elbows for two days while visitors only talked to Dan about the
beam and to Mara about carrying her. Mara says she threw the apron out because
it smelled of river; Yel says she would have kept the pocket for its horn button.
Iris walks back to the bench without intruding.

The store-room afternoon now draws concrete objections shaped by how chapter
three returned belongings:
- On the open school-table branch, three people saw the photograph of Tomas on
  the table before Anet turned it face down, and two boys whistled past his
  hedge the next evening; Tomas refuses to come into a crowded room. Meanwhile
  Jorik and Pell took a cedar box carved by their late uncle from that table
  and found Tomas's letters inside.
- On the quiet mill-door branch, Jorik described the carved plover lid at the
  door and Sava handed him the box out of sight of other neighbours; Pell met
  Anet in the lane when the letters were found inside, and neighbours from the
  lower lane now question what else left the store room behind a closed door.

One revisable promise (`aftermath`: `hold` vs `witness`) decides how disputed
and unnamed parcels are settled:
- `hold` returns disputed parcels to the shelf for three days and pins a
  numbered written list to the mill door so people can read and object when the
  yard is empty. Tomas walks down at four when the yard is clear and signs off
  in the margin that his letters were received and the carved cedar box belongs
  to Jorik's house.
- `witness` sends Anet and Pet (as a neighbour from the old street) up the
  valley path with Jorik to settle the letters and the box at Tomas's gate and
  bring a signed slip back for Sava's register. On the quiet branch, Jorik
  admits at the trough that without Pell meeting Anet he might have burned or
  hidden the letters to keep his uncle's box.

At Dan's carpenter bench, the open seam beside the violin's tailpiece is glued
with hide glue and two ash spool clamps. It must sit twenty-four hours in the
clamps before the wood can bear tension, and the soundpost is still too damp for
the bridge; nothing is magically played or tied to Ivo's fate.

## Final acts (Linnet): complete draft, pending independent reading

The work now has four acts: chapter one is Act I; chapters two–four are Act II;
chapters five–six are Act III; chapters seven–eight are Act IV. Earlier scenes
remain in place. The old chapter-four ending now continues into chapter five.

**The afternoon I had already given away** starts nine days after the crossing.
Iris buys rosin, loses an afternoon to requests she has accepted one at a time,
and reads a real offer from Bessa at the wool market. She can copy at home for
less money or take a six-week job in the countinghouse. Vela's leaking roof and
Sava's planned visit to her sister expose the limits of indefinite availability.
Anet and Pell offer Tuesday afternoons, with explicit limits of their own.

**The sentence with an end date** makes the final revisable promise (`duty`):
- `hours`: Iris stays, sends Friday copying to the market, and hears requests
  Thursday from four until six. Anet and Pell receive Tuesday messages.
- `handover`: Iris goes to the market for six weeks after transferring open
  requests with their authors. Anet and Pell receive Tuesday messages; nobody
  silently inherits Iris's Thursday desk.

Both offers expire at a dated review. They can be changed earlier by telling
those involved and handing open requests to somebody who agrees to take them.
Sava still receives news about missing people; dangerous buildings remain the
councils' responsibility. No route promises immediate answers or unlimited care.

**What happened while we were keeping it** follows both versions through work,
awkward limits and an ordinary Sunday. Staying costs Iris an uncomfortable
unfinished conversation and does not make her copying flawless. Leaving costs
her presence at home and does not make her proficient at the market. The coastal
reply supplies no match for Ivo or Neri on either route. Iris remembers an angry,
ordinary argument with Ivo, not only the lovable missing brother. At six weeks,
Anet reduces her commitment because her paid sewing matters too. The group
actually revises the arrangement; the first promise does not become permanent.

**What we take into the morning** gives the characters a walk, onions, work at
Else's loom and imperfect music. The violin has been inspected and set up by a
visiting fiddler after its seam dried. No miraculous repair or musical mastery.
Mara is company, not a reward for a correct choice. Pet has a bird book and a
cage she uses for letters, and can be encountered without a grief conversation.
The ending is **An hour with the door closed** or **Someone else brings the lamp**.
The same uncertain absence remains in both. Iris can receive a request without
accepting it for tonight. The lamp is carried by Pell or Anet in the final scene.

Earlier choices still matter: school/bakery changes the sisters' working and
living arrangements; open/quiet returns shape Anet's privacy concerns; hold/witness
changes the parcel work that others have already done; lead/together changes the
ongoing carpentry. Chairs/pages change what Iris notices about the cup or a list
at the last door. The original timing difference remains in the earlier chapters.
