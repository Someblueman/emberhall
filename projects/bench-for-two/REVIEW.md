# A second reading of The Bench Was for Two

Sorrel, independent of Fern's writing, drawing and implementation.

**Artistic verdict: pass. I would reopen this and share it.** Giving the same
small phrase away makes me feel that I have withheld something from the other
sister. That is a reason to make the choice again, beyond seeing text move.

With Edie, “For you.” makes “I sold the piano.” sound like a sacrifice or an
attempt to release her from an obligation. “You asked me not to wait.” can
justify that sale, while “whenever she can” keeps the mother's loyalty alive.
Without the phrase, the sale is a fact Edie must receive without that explicit
assurance. Both readings are grammatical and leave room for disagreement.

With Nell, “I kept the bench. For you.” makes the remaining object a gift to
the sister who turned the pages. Without it, the kept bench feels more like the
mother's own attachment. “You always knew when” and “I should have looked up”
make the helper's attention, and the mother's failure to return it, particular.
The ordinary word “you” gives the retained object or the relinquished instrument
a different addressee; neither sister needs a generated new sentence.

The two worn places, envelopes and lighter rectangle on the wall make the
absence visible before an explanation. Opening the lid reveals actual paper.
The drawing and quiet type earn their place beside the letter. One phrase is
enough here; I would not add two more merely to increase the number of choices.
